The big idea

A sign of the times.

Flock's fixed readers are designed to capture vehicle details, location, and time, then make those records searchable across an expanding network.

The real pushback is bigger than a cardboard sign: open-source camera maps, public-records requests, council meetings, contract challenges, and demands for warrants, audits, and local control.

The sign is the visible part of an argument about surveillance most drivers never see.

Street-level signal

Make it visible.

A camera on a pole can look like ordinary roadside hardware. A person with a sign turns an invisible data system into a public question.

“If a camera can document a community, a community can document the camera.”— The street-level oversight principle

What the evidence supports

Straight answers.

What is a Flock camera?

Flock Safety sells automated license-plate readers that capture vehicle information and make it searchable. The company says its fixed cameras are solar-powered, LTE-connected, and designed for real-time alerts and investigations.

What does the broader movement actually do?

The documented opposition is centered on camera maps, public-records work, city-council pressure, contract challenges, lawsuits, and demands for warrants and enforceable limits. AP reported that more than 50 agencies or jurisdictions had canceled, suspended, rejected, or deactivated contracts or cameras since the start of 2026, based on DeFlock's tracker.

Do cardboard signs stop the network?

A sign may interrupt one camera's view for a moment, but it does not disable a network, erase earlier records, or stop other readers. We found strong evidence of a national anti-ALPR movement, but not reliable evidence of a large coordinated campaign built around people continuously blocking cameras with cardboard.

Why are people concerned?

Critics point to the scale of collection, cross-jurisdiction sharing, sensitive-location tracking, immigration access, and documented unauthorized searches. Police and Flock say the technology helps recover stolen vehicles, find missing people, and develop leads in serious cases.

What is the lawful point?

Make the system visible. Ask who approved it, what policy governs searches, how long records are kept, which agencies can access them, and whether the public receives audit reports. Do not trespass, obstruct roads, touch equipment, or interfere with infrastructure.
